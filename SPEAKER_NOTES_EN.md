# Complete English Speaker Notes
## Approximately 45 minutes / 20 slides

> Delivery note: The script is intentionally conversational. At a normal academic-conference pace, allow 2–3 minutes per slide, with brief pauses for figures and transitions. Replace bracketed placeholders before delivery.

## Slide 1 — Opening

Good morning/afternoon, distinguished colleagues and friends.

It is a pleasure to introduce the National Institute of Chemistry of Slovenia, commonly known as NIC, or *Kemijski inštitut*.

For many members of the international scientific and industrial community, Slovenia may still be relatively unfamiliar. Yet this small Central European country is well connected to European research and innovation networks, and it has a strong tradition of scientific work.

Today I would like to introduce both the institute and the opportunities it may offer to universities, research organizations and companies looking for international partners.

The central message of this presentation is simple: NIC is a compact but internationally oriented research institution that connects chemistry with materials science, energy, biotechnology, environmental research and industrial innovation.

## Slide 2 — Why Slovenia?

Let me first provide a short national context.

Slovenia is located at the intersection of Central Europe, the Alpine region, the Adriatic area and Southeast Europe. This position gives the country a natural connection to several scientific and industrial ecosystems.

As a member of the European Union and the European Research Area, Slovenia participates in European research programmes, infrastructure networks and innovation initiatives.

For international partners, the country’s scale can be an advantage. Research communities are relatively compact, communication channels can be direct, and cooperation between universities, public institutes and companies can often be developed efficiently.

In this environment, NIC acts as one of the country’s key scientific and technological institutions. It provides a point of contact between national expertise and broader European collaboration.

## Slide 3 — What Is NIC?

The National Institute of Chemistry is Slovenia’s leading public research institution focused on chemistry and chemistry-related fields.

Its work extends well beyond traditional chemistry. Researchers work in materials science, analytical chemistry, biotechnology, life sciences, chemical engineering, energy, environmental protection, structural chemistry and theoretical chemistry.

This breadth is important because many major challenges cannot be solved by one discipline. Energy storage requires chemistry, materials science, engineering and analysis. Drug development requires molecular biology, structural information, analytical methods and biotechnology. Environmental technologies require chemistry, process engineering and systems thinking.

NIC’s interdisciplinary structure allows researchers to approach such challenges from several directions at once.

## Slide 4 — From Fundamental Science to Innovation

A useful way to understand the institute is to view its work as a continuum.

At one end is fundamental understanding: molecular structures, mechanisms, interfaces and physical properties. Next come molecular and materials design, advanced characterization, prototype development, process engineering and validation.

At the other end is transfer into practical applications.

This does not mean that every project must become a product. Fundamental research remains essential. But the institute’s capabilities make it possible to connect fundamental discoveries with applied questions when a project requires it.

For academic partners, this can support joint publications, doctoral projects and new scientific directions. For companies, it can provide access to expertise and equipment at stages before full commercial deployment.

## Slide 5 — Main Research Areas

NIC’s portfolio covers several mutually reinforcing areas.

These include advanced materials and nanotechnology; energy storage and conversion; catalysis and chemical engineering; analytical and structural chemistry; biotechnology and life sciences; environmental protection and circular economy; and technologies related to gene and cell therapy.

The important point is not simply the number of topics. It is the possibility of combining them.

For example, a battery project may require materials synthesis, microscopy, spectroscopy, electrochemistry and engineering. A biotechnology project may require molecular design, analytical chemistry, protein science and process development.

The most interesting opportunities for international cooperation often arise at these interfaces.

## Slide 6 — Advanced Materials and Nanotechnology

Advanced materials are foundations of many future technologies.

Research in this area asks how materials can be designed for higher performance, how their structure can be controlled at the nanoscale, how surfaces and interfaces can be modified, and how these changes affect real performance.

Relevant systems may include functional materials, nanostructured materials, polymers, hybrid materials and catalytic materials.

The key scientific relationship is the relationship between structure and property. A material’s performance may depend on atomic arrangement, defects, particle size, surface chemistry or interfaces between phases.

For academic partners, this creates opportunities for joint work on synthesis, theory, characterization and mechanisms. For companies, applications may include catalysts, coatings, membranes, sensors, specialty polymers and energy materials.

## Slide 7 — Energy, Batteries and Sustainable Technologies

The energy transition is an especially important area for cooperation between science and industry.

Developing a better battery involves much more than finding a promising material. Researchers must understand electrochemical mechanisms, degradation, safety, lifetime, manufacturing conditions and cost.

NIC’s combination of chemistry, materials science, electrochemistry and characterization provides a strong basis for research in energy storage and related sustainable technologies.

This is a field where academic and industrial interests are naturally aligned. Universities may contribute mechanisms and models. Research institutes may contribute materials design, testing and advanced analysis. Companies may contribute manufacturing knowledge, engineering constraints and market requirements.

The strongest projects connect these contributions from material discovery to device-level validation and scale-up.

## Slide 8 — Biotechnology and Life Sciences

A second major dimension is the interface between chemistry and biology.

Modern life-science research depends on chemical knowledge. Biological systems must be understood at molecular level, therapeutic compounds must be designed and delivered effectively, and biological processes must be controlled and optimized.

Relevant activities include molecular biology, protein engineering, genomics, drug delivery, gene and cell therapy technologies and bioprocessing.

This makes NIC relevant to universities and companies working in pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, diagnostics and medical technologies.

The interdisciplinary environment is particularly valuable. Chemists, biologists, engineers and other specialists can work around a common problem rather than developing isolated solutions.

## Slide 9 — Environmental Protection and Circular Economy

Environmental protection is now a central requirement for technological competitiveness.

The scientific questions include how pollutants can be detected, how waste streams can become sources of valuable materials, how processes can consume less energy and resources, and how the total environmental footprint of a technology can be reduced.

Circular economy research requires cooperation across the value chain. Researchers need to understand chemical transformations and material properties. Companies need to redesign products, processes and supply chains.

Potential cooperation areas include green chemistry, environmental monitoring, resource recovery, waste valorization and sustainable materials.

This is also an area in which international partnerships can be highly valuable, because environmental challenges and industrial value chains cross national borders.

## Slide 10 — Advanced Research Infrastructure

High-quality science requires both good ideas and appropriate infrastructure.

NIC has access to advanced platforms supporting molecular, materials and biological research. These include high-field NMR, electron microscopy, cryo-electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction and advanced analytical and surface-characterization methods.

Together, these tools allow researchers to study systems across length scales, from atoms and molecules to nanoscale morphology and macroscopic performance.

For international partners, specialized infrastructure can be a direct reason to collaborate. A university or company may have a promising sample, material or biological system but lack access to a particular technique or the expertise required to interpret the results.

The value of the infrastructure is therefore not only the instrument. It is the combination of instrument, method development and scientific interpretation.

## Slide 11 — The Slovenian NMR Centre

Nuclear magnetic resonance is important because it can reveal molecular structure, dynamics, interactions and reaction behavior.

High-field NMR supports research in organic chemistry, catalysis, materials, pharmaceuticals, biological macromolecules and reaction mechanisms.

For academic partners, it can provide a shared platform for investigating molecules, materials and biological systems. For industry, it can support formulation studies, impurity analysis, reaction monitoring, quality-related questions and technology development.

A successful NMR collaboration depends on more than acquiring a spectrum. It requires an appropriately designed experiment and an interpretation connected to the scientific or industrial decision that must be made.

This is a good example of how infrastructure and expertise work together.

## Slide 12 — Electron Microscopy and Materials Characterization

Bulk measurements alone are often insufficient to explain why a material performs as it does.

Electron microscopy can reveal morphology, interfaces, defects, particles and nanostructures. Elemental analysis can show local chemical composition. High-resolution techniques can connect atomic structure to macroscopic properties.

These capabilities are relevant to catalysts, battery materials, nanomaterials, membranes, polymers and biological systems.

The lifetime of a battery, for example, may depend on nanoscale changes at an interface. The performance of a catalyst may depend on the distribution of active sites. The behavior of a biomedical material may depend on its surface and interaction with biological components.

Advanced microscopy is therefore most valuable when integrated into a complete research workflow.

## Slide 13 — People, Training and the Next Generation

Research collaboration is ultimately collaboration between people.

NIC contributes to the training of young researchers and doctoral students. This matters because international projects require researchers who are comfortable working across disciplines, institutions and national borders.

Possible forms of cooperation include joint doctoral supervision, student and postdoctoral exchanges, short research visits, shared experimental training, summer schools and laboratory internships.

For universities, these activities strengthen doctoral education and international networks. For companies, they help identify and train future scientific and technological talent.

A good partnership should therefore not be limited to one experiment or one publication. It should build a durable community of researchers.

## Slide 14 — A Small Institute with International Reach

An important question is how a relatively compact institute can achieve international relevance.

The answer lies in concentration and connectivity: specialized expertise, advanced infrastructure, interdisciplinary cooperation and participation in international networks.

NIC connects national scientific capacity with European research opportunities. It can function as a research partner, an infrastructure partner, a training partner and, in appropriate cases, a technology-transfer partner.

Its scale may also support flexibility and direct communication. Of course, a specific collaboration still requires discussion with the relevant research group. But the institute’s profile makes it a potentially valuable node in larger international projects.

## Slide 15 — Cooperation with Industry

For industry leaders, the practical question is: what can cooperation deliver?

Possible models include contract research, joint R&D, technology validation, analytical and testing services, prototype development, scale-up support, licensing and intellectual-property cooperation.

The right model depends on technology maturity, confidentiality requirements, investment horizon and the capabilities of each partner.

A company may begin with a defined analytical or materials problem. If the results are promising, the collaboration may develop into a joint development programme. A more mature technology may require validation, prototype work or European innovation funding.

The objective is to align scientific ambition with a clearly defined industrial question.

## Slide 16 — Why International Collaboration Matters

International collaboration creates value when partners contribute complementary capabilities.

Universities often contribute fundamental knowledge, theories, models and doctoral talent. NIC may contribute interdisciplinary integration, specialized infrastructure, applied research and validation. Industry contributes market needs, engineering experience, manufacturing knowledge and scale-up capability.

The purpose is not merely to exchange samples or publications. It is to create capabilities that no single partner could develop as efficiently alone.

This is particularly important in batteries, sustainable chemistry, biotechnology, advanced materials and environmental technologies, where the path from discovery to application crosses institutional boundaries.

## Slide 17 — Potential China–Slovenia Cooperation Areas

For Chinese universities and companies, several areas may be promising.

These include advanced materials, battery materials and energy storage, green chemistry, circular economy technologies, catalysis, low-carbon processes, biotechnology and advanced analytical methods.

Potential formats include joint publications, co-supervised doctoral projects, researcher exchanges, shared laboratories, joint funding applications, technology licensing and company–institute R&D agreements.

The goal should not be cooperation for its own sake. The goal should be to identify a problem where each side brings a capability that the other does not easily possess.

That is the basis for durable and meaningful cooperation.

## Slide 18 — A Practical Roadmap

How should a partnership begin?

First, identify a shared challenge rather than making only a general statement of interest. Second, match research groups, facilities and technical capabilities. Third, conduct a feasibility study, perhaps through sample exchange, preliminary experiments or a short research visit.

Fourth, agree early on intellectual property, data ownership, confidentiality and publication principles. Fifth, launch a focused pilot with measurable outcomes. Finally, if the pilot succeeds, expand into a larger consortium, joint laboratory, doctoral programme or strategic partnership.

This gradual approach reduces risk while building trust.

## Slide 19 — Questions for Potential Partners

I would like to leave six questions for discussion.

Which scientific challenges require complementary expertise? Which facilities or analytical methods are difficult to access independently? Which technologies are approaching industrial deployment? Where could joint doctoral training add value? Which European or international funding programmes could support the work? And what would be a realistic and scientifically meaningful first pilot project?

These questions can turn general interest into a concrete plan.

## Slide 20 — Closing

To conclude, the National Institute of Chemistry of Slovenia is more than a national research institution.

It is a platform where chemistry connects with materials science, energy, biotechnology, environmental research and industrial innovation.

For universities, NIC may offer joint research, doctoral training, shared infrastructure and scientific exchange. For companies, it may offer scientific expertise, technology validation, analytical capabilities and pathways toward innovation.

The institute’s message to the international community can be summarized in one sentence: small in scale, international in ambition, and broad in scientific capability.

I hope this presentation encourages new conversations and helps identify concrete opportunities for cooperation.

Thank you very much. I look forward to your questions and to future discussions.
